From the question to the conclusion.

Using only what is known on 31 March 2017, can we predict which KKBOX subscribers whose plan expires in March 2017 will leave — and does what they listen to say more than what they pay?

Target is_churn: no new valid subscription within 30 days of that plan's expiry. Unit: one subscriber, 946,686 of them; 9% churn. The test split was scored once.

Main setting · cutoff 31 March

Profile, billing up to 31 March, March listening. An upper bound: most labelled plans have already expired, so the billing record often already shows the renewal.

Stricter setting · billing cut at 1 March

Profile, billing from before 1 March (before any labelled plan expires), March listening. Closer to an early warning, but listening still runs to 31 March.

Never used

Anything dated after 31 March 2017, and the label itself. Every choice is made on validation; test is scored once.

Scope of the claim. One cohort of one service (KKBOX plans expiring in March 2017), a random split within that month, and association, not cause: the scores say who is likely to leave, not why, and not how a later month would look.

01Why/ 16

Why predict churn, and why this question

A music subscription earns money only while people keep renewing. If a service can tell before a plan runs out who is likely to leave, it can spend its retention budget (reminders, discounts, offers) on those subscribers instead of on everyone.

Why early

A warning is only useful while there is still time to act. That is why every feature is built from data up to a fixed cutoff. Our main cutoff, 31 March, comes after most labelled plans have expired, so its score is an upper bound; we therefore also cut billing at 1 March, before any labelled plan expires (listening still covers March).

Why listening, not just billing

Billing records (auto-renew, cancellations, expiry) are what a business already watches. Listening shows engagement: someone who stops playing music may leave even while auto-renew is on. The question is whether that behaviour adds a signal billing does not already have.

Why it is hard

Only 9% of subscribers churn, so a model can look accurate while catching nobody. Many profiles are incomplete (60% of ages missing), and the billing window overlaps the label window, which can make the task look easier than it is.

Why one row per subscriber, and why a cutoff date

The business question is who to contact, so the unit is a subscriber, not a transaction or a day. Each table is aggregated up to 31 March 2017. Anything dated later is information the business does not have when it must decide, so using it would make the scores look better than they are in practice.

The cutoff alone is not enough here: plans expire during March, so some renewals are already in March billing. That is why notebook 08 repeats everything with a 1 March cutoff.

Why missing values are kept, not dropped

Missing data is not random in this dataset. A subscriber with no transactions churns at , against 9% overall (training split). Dropping them would remove the easiest and most important cases and bias every score.

So we keep everyone and add three flags (has_member, has_tx, has_logs). Logistic regression fills gaps with the training median; LightGBM learns which branch missing values should follow.

Why log-scaling only for logistic regression

Hours listened and amount paid are heavily right-skewed: a few subscribers have huge values. A linear model would let those dominate, so we apply log1p and standardise.

Trees only ask "is x above some split point?", and a log does not change the order of values, so trees get the raw values.

Split and metrics

70 / 10 / 20Split
Stratified random split, seed 42: 70 % train (fit), 10 % validation (model choice, tuning, threshold) and 20 % test (used once). Churn rate is in every part. Split table →
PSI < 0.001Distribution shift
To check that the splits really come from the same population, each numeric feature in validation, test and the Kaggle test set is compared with the training split using the population stability index (PSI; above 0.1 usually means a shift worth investigating). The largest value over 30 features is 0.0007 (complete_ratio, Kaggle test), so validation and test scores carry over to the Kaggle test subscribers. Shift chart →
PR-AUCMain metric
Churners are only 9 % of subscribers, so PR-AUC is the main metric; its no-skill level is 0.09. ROC-AUC, log loss and Brier are reported alongside, and log loss is also the tuning objective. Precision, recall, F1 and accuracy are taken at the decision threshold; accuracy is read against the 0.91 of the majority baseline.
τ = Threshold
To flag subscribers we need a cut-off on the probability. We pick the one that maximises F1 on validation, then apply it unchanged to test. F1 weighs a missed churner and a false alarm equally. A business that values a lost subscriber more than a wasted offer should lower τ to raise recall; the cost explorer shows how the best τ moves with that ratio. Any such choice should be made on validation, not test.
1 MarStrict check
Because March transactions overlap the label window, the tuned LightGBM is retrained with billing features from before 1 March 2017 only, on the same split, and scored once on the same test subscribers. It keeps the hyperparameters tuned for 31 March (only the number of trees and the threshold are re-chosen on validation), so if anything its score is slightly pessimistic. Results →
1 000Bootstrap resamples
The champion's test metrics get 95 % intervals from 1 000 bootstrap resamples of the test set. Differences between models use a paired bootstrap (both models scored on the same resample): 1 000 resamples for the tuned LightGBM against each other model, 500 for the feature-group ablation and the strict check. This shows how much a number could move with another sample of test subscribers. It does not cover training variance (each model is trained once, with one seed), how the model would do in a later month, or why subscribers churn.
1 splitReused validation
The same validation split is used for early stopping, the 30 Optuna trials, the model choice and the threshold. Picking the best of many tries on one split makes its score slightly optimistic, which is why every learned model scores a little lower on test (see Does it hold up?). The test split, used once, is the score to quote.

How to read the intervals

A bracket like 0.9644 [0.9624, 0.9664] is a 95% bootstrap interval: if we drew another test set of the same size from the same population, the score would land in this range about 95% of the time. For a difference between two models (for example "+0.0083 [0.0073, 0.0093]"), the rule is simple: if the whole interval is above zero, the second model is reliably better; if it crosses zero, one test set cannot tell them apart.

PR-AUC instead of accuracy

With 9% churners, predicting "nobody churns" is 91% accurate and completely useless. Accuracy rewards that.

PR-AUC looks only at how well the model ranks churners: at each cut-off, what share of flagged subscribers really churn (precision) and what share of churners are caught (recall). Its no-skill level equals the churn rate, 0.09, so the scale is honest for imbalanced data (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015).

Bootstrap and paired bootstrap

Bootstrap: draw 189,338 test subscribers with replacement from the test set, recompute the score, repeat 1,000 times, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

Paired: to compare two models, both are scored on the same resample and we keep the difference. Subscribers that are hard for one model are usually hard for the other, so pairing cancels that shared noise and gives a much tighter interval for the difference than comparing two separate intervals.

Choosing the threshold on validation

The model gives probabilities; contacting someone needs a yes or no. We tried thresholds from 0.01 to 0.97 on the validation split and kept the one with the highest F1 (0.45).

Picking it on test would make the test F1 optimistic, because the threshold would be fitted to those exact subscribers.

Test split used once

Every decision (features, models, hyperparameters, threshold, feature sets) was made on training and validation data. The test split was scored once, at the end, in notebook 06. That is what makes its numbers an estimate for new subscribers, not a record of our tuning.

05Baseline and methods/ 16

From a constant guess to tuned boosting

Every model outputs a churn probability and, apart from the baselines, is trained by minimising log loss on the training split. Every hyperparameter was chosen on the validation split; the test split played no part.

Why these three model families

After the two baselines, each model tests one idea about the data. Logistic regression (linear) asks whether a straight-line boundary is enough. Random forest (bagged trees) asks whether thresholds and interactions matter, such as auto-renew off and a plan about to expire. LightGBM (boosted trees) asks whether trees that correct each other do better still; it is usually the strongest family for tabular data with missing values and categories. Together they cover the three main families for tabular classification, and the gains level off (tuning adds only +0.0025 PR-AUC), so another family is unlikely to change the conclusions.

Ablation. To answer the second half of the question, the tuned LightGBM is retrained on three feature sets: billing + demographics, listening + demographics, and all features.

Why baselines first

A score like PR-AUC 0.96 means nothing on its own. The majority baseline shows the no-skill level (0.0899, the churn rate). The auto-renew rule shows what a business gets from one flag and no model (0.3164).

Every model in the ladder has to beat the one below it. If a complex model lost to the rule, that would point to a bug, not a finding.

Gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM)

Boosting builds trees one after another. Each new tree is fitted to the errors (the gradient of log loss) of all the trees before it, and its output is added with a small weight, the learning rate.

  • num_leaves = 50: how complex each tree can be.
  • learning rate 0.067, 861 trees: small steps, many trees.
  • row / column sampling and L2 penalty: add randomness and shrinkage so trees do not memorise the training set.

This suits churn because the signal is in thresholds (days until expiry) and interactions (auto-renew off and plan about to expire).

Optuna hyperparameter search

Instead of a fixed grid, Optuna's TPE sampler looks at the trials so far and proposes new settings near the ones that did well. We ran 30 trials (seeded, so the search is repeatable), each scored by validation log loss, and kept the best (trial 14, log loss 0.0423). The six searched settings and their ranges are in the table above; each trial also used early stopping on validation.

The test split was never used, so it stays a fair, unseen check.

Why models are chosen by log loss, not PR-AUC

PR-AUC is how we report ranking quality, but we select and tune by validation log loss. Log loss is a proper scoring rule: it is lowest only when the predicted probabilities are honest, and a business needs honest probabilities to set a threshold or weigh costs. It is also smooth, which suits Optuna and early stopping, while PR-AUC depends only on the order of the scores.

The two agree here: the model with the best validation log loss also has the best validation PR-AUC (0.9686).

Considered, but not used
  • Survival (time-to-event) models: they need a time axis per subscriber; here the outcome is one fixed 30-day window after one expiry.
  • Feature selection: skipped on purpose, so no choice depends on held-out data; trees ignore unhelpful features anyway.
Why we did not reweight the classes

Only 9% of subscribers churn. Reweighting churners would raise recall but distort the probabilities: a predicted 30% would no longer mean that 30% of such subscribers churn.

We want honest probabilities (checked in the calibration plot), and we handle the imbalance where it belongs: in the metric (PR-AUC) and in the decision threshold.

06Leakage/ 16

Could the model be seeing the answer?

Leakage is when a feature carries information the model would not have at prediction time, or carries the label itself. It makes scores look better than they will be in use. We checked each way it could enter this project.

Why the 31 March score is optimistic

A subscriber churns if they do not renew within 30 days of their plan's expiry, and every labelled plan expires in March 2017. 95.4% of subscribers have a March transaction, and they churn at only 5.5%; the rest churn at 80.9%.

So by 31 March, a renewal is often already visible in billing. This is not leakage from the future (it is dated before the cutoff), but it means the model partly reads the decision instead of predicting it.

What the strict check changes, and what it does not

Notebook 08 rebuilds every billing feature from transactions dated before 1 March 2017, before any labelled expiry, and retrains the same tuned LightGBM on the same split. The threshold is re-chosen on validation.

Listening still covers all of March, so some of it may fall after a subscriber's expiry. Part of listening's +0.1474 may therefore still reflect the outcome.

09Failure modes/ 16

Where the champion fails, and why

At τ = 0.45 the champion misses 1,484 of 17,029 test churners and raises 2,063 false alarms. These come mostly from four groups.

1. Plans expiring soon after the cutoff

recall 0.595

158,450 test subscribers have a plan expiring within 30 days, and only 1.85% of them churn. The renewal has not happened yet, so billing cannot show it. 1,187 of the 1,484 missed churners are here.

2. Auto-renew still on

36.6% of misses

Over a third of missed churners still had auto-renew switched on. Their billing looks exactly like a loyal subscriber's, and the model leans on billing.

3. Subscribers with no transactions

1,233 false alarms

7,248 subscribers have no billing history and 78% of them churn, so the model flags almost all of them. The 22% who stay make up most of the false alarms.

4. Active listeners who still leave

recall 0.892

Recall is 0.892 for subscribers with March listening, against 0.985 for those without. Someone who keeps playing music but does not renew gives the model little to go on.

Per-segment numbers and examples are in Errors. A time-based split, cutting listening at each expiry, and a model for soon-expiring plans are listed under What next.

10Runtime model/ 16

The real model, running in your browser

The Model Predictor page does not call a server and does not use a simplified copy. It runs the exact tuned LightGBM from models/lgbm_champion.txt in JavaScript.

861Trees exported
Notebook 07 writes every tree (split features, thresholds, categorical sets, leaf values) to js/generated/model_lgbm.js (2.9 MB). js/sandbox_engine.js walks each tree the same way LightGBM does, including its rules for missing values and categories, sums the leaves and applies the sigmoid.
16Inputs asked

The form asks for 16 of the 33 features: the ones a person can actually know about a subscriber, such as plan length, last payment, auto-renew, days to expiry, March listening and age. Asking for all of them would mean typing in a full payment history (number of payments, past cancellations, share of auto-renew payments, discounts, payment methods), which nobody has to hand.

The other 17 are handled like this: 3 presence flags are always 1; 6 are worked out from the inputs (for example, days since the last payment = plan length − days to expiry); about 8 history features are filled with the training median of subscribers with the same auto-renew and cancel status. Those 8 carry about 5% of the model's split gain, so the prediction is close to the real model's answer, but it illustrates the model rather than scoring a real subscriber. The page also shows how much each feature pushed the prediction up or down: along each tree's path, the change in node value at every split is credited to that split's feature, so the contributions plus a base value add up exactly to the log-odds (checked in website/tests/check_model_parity.mjs).

11Limitations/ 16

What these results do not show

The 31 March scores are optimistic for early warning

KKBOX defines churn from transactions, and the labelled plans expire during March, so the last March transaction often already shows the renewal decision. The headline PR-AUC means "easy once billing shows the decision", not "we can read intentions". With billing cut at 1 March the model is much weaker (see the strict check).

One snapshot, random split

Train and test subscribers come from the same month. We do not show that the model keeps working for a later month, when prices, promotions or user mix may change. A split by time was not possible: the dataset labels only one cohort (plans expiring in March 2017), and the listening logs cover that month only. So we used a stratified random split by subscriber, which keeps each subscriber in one split and the churn rate equal across splits, and we state that the scores hold for this month’s population, not for future months.

Hard cases stay hard

Prediction is not causation

Feature importance shows what the model uses, not what makes people leave. For example, switching a subscriber's auto-renew on would not necessarily keep them: auto-renew is partly a sign of an earlier decision to stay. Testing an intervention would need an experiment.

Listening data is thin

Only March 2017 is used, and about a fifth of subscribers have no logs at all. A longer listening history might show a larger effect than our ablation finds.

Threshold and tuning use the same validation split

This makes validation scores slightly optimistic; test scores are not affected because the test split was never used for any choice.

Predictor page is approximate

The predictor runs the real champion model, but asks for 16 inputs; the other features are filled with training medians for similar subscribers. Treat it as an illustration of the model, not a scoring tool. Demographic features (age, city) also raise fairness questions if used for decisions.

Privacy and fairness

Subscriber IDs are anonymised hashes and there are no names or contact details. But age and gender are model inputs, and we did not check whether error rates differ between age or gender groups. Before any real use, audit those groups, and use the scores to offer help to people likely to leave, not to treat them worse.

The Kaggle test set has no labels

The competition test file lists subscribers without the answer. We predict them (results/submission.csv) but cannot score them here, so every number on this page comes from our own labelled test split.

12Conclusion/ 16

Answer to the question

Which number to quote

0.9644 is an upper bound: at 31 March the billing record often already shows whether the subscriber renewed, so it measures how well the model reads that record, not how early it can warn. 0.5682 [0.5610, 0.5757] is the closer-to-early-warning score: billing from before 1 March, before any labelled plan expires, plus March listening (6.3× the no-skill level). It is still not a pure 1 March prediction, because the listening features run to 31 March; a true one would cut listening too (see What next).

13What next/ 16

What we would do next

Split by time, not at random

Train on earlier months and test on a later one, to see how the model holds up as behaviour and prices drift.

Cut listening at each subscriber's expiry

So the strict check has no post-expiry signal at all, and the listening gain at 1 March can be read cleanly.

Choose the threshold by cost

Set τ from the real cost of a lost subscriber versus a wasted offer, instead of F1, which weighs them equally.

A model for soon-expiring plans

Plans expiring within 30 days of the cutoff are where recall drops to 0.595; a model trained only on them could use listening trends more.

04Data quality/ 09

What is missing or wrong, and what we did

Most gaps come from a subscriber being absent from a table. Absence is informative here (for example, subscribers without any transaction churn far more often), so we keep these subscribers and add a flag instead of dropping or blindly filling them.

Issues found

  • Age (bd) is unusable for most members. % of member rows have age 0, negative or above 90. We treat ages outside 10–90 as missing.
  • Gender is mostly blank (% of member rows). We encode it as female / male / not given.
  • No member profile for % of labelled subscribers → has_member = 0; demographics left missing.
  • No transactions for % of subscribers. These churn at → has_tx = 0, counts set to 0, “last transaction” fields left missing.
  • No March listening logs for of subscribers → has_logs = 0, listening totals set to 0, days since last listen = 31.
  • Impossible listening times: daily log rows have total_secs below 0 or above 86 400 (one day); clipped to [0, 86 400].
  • Heavy skew in money and volume columns (skewness of tx_count = ): log1p + scaling for logistic regression only; trees do not need it.

Missing values left after the joins

% of labelled subscribers. Remaining gaps are imputed inside each model pipeline (median, learned on the training split only) or handled natively by LightGBM.
